Why this shape may suit Cambodia

Not because blockchain is fashionable — because these are ordinary national problems.
  • The unit is the riel. No exposure to a foreign coin's price, and nothing to speculate on.
  • Privacy for people, transparency for public money. Your balance is not world-readable; the public fund's every riel is open to anyone with no login.
  • Losing your phone is not losing your money. Identity-backed recovery reissues to a new address under the same registered person.
  • Assistance that reaches its purpose. A food grant can be spent at licensed food merchants and refused elsewhere — by the chain, not by a report written months later.
  • Land titles that other builders can use. Only a regulated registrar can issue one; any lender can then verify it without asking permission.
  • Cheap by design. About 1 riel per payment, and that riel funds a public good rather than being burned.
  • Value cannot quietly leave. One governed gate out, with minimum tiers, daily caps and a stop switch — capital rules written as code instead of paperwork.

Built for the threats that are coming

Two failure modes will define the next decade of financial infrastructure. A sovereign, permissioned chain can answer both in ways a public chain and a traditional bank structurally cannot.

🛡 AI-driven attack

The realistic AI attack is not breaking cryptography. It is convincingly impersonating an official, at scale, until someone approves something.
  • Nobody can act alone. Every administrative power is held by institutional multisigs.
  • Powers are split. Compromise one authority and you get one power, not the chain.
  • Every freeze carries a court-order reference — an action without one is impossible, not merely wrong.
  • A stolen key is not a lost account. The attacker's advantage expires.
Honestly: resilience, not immunity. One successful deception is not enough to move money, and the attempt is visible to parties the attacker does not control.

🔐 Quantum computers

Every blockchain today relies on ECDSA signatures. A quantum computer breaks them — and on a public ledger, the keys it will break are already published, waiting.
  • Accounts can change their cryptography — same address, no need to move anyone's money.
  • A governed validator set can actually execute the upgrade. On a public chain it is a years-long negotiation that may simply fail.
  • “Private to the world” means far less to harvest today and decrypt later.
Honestly: post-quantum signatures are not deployed anywhere yet — the tooling is not ready. This is quantum readiness, not quantum proof. Anyone claiming the latter today should be read carefully.
