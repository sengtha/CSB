lock riel, issue dollars — the riel is the collateral, not the peg
The exchange rate here is published by hand. CSB has no market to read a rate from, so every price below comes from the administered oracle — a number a role posts, with a citation, not an observation of trading. The collateral arithmetic is real; the valuation underneath it is only as good as that number, which is why each currency has a hard cap on how much can ever be issued. Borrow against riel

Lock first, then issue. Both are ordinary transactions and both can be undone: repay what you issued and the riel comes back. There is no interest — this vault charges none, so a position left alone stays exactly where you left it unless the published rate moves.

Each currency has its own position. Riel locked for khUSD does not back khJPY — switching the selector above switches which position every button acts on. Positions are kept apart so a rate that moves against one currency cannot pull the others down with it.

Positions at risk

Anyone holding the currency may close an undercollateralised position, repaying part of its debt and taking that much collateral plus the penalty. Not a privilege of this page — it is what liquidate() does for any caller.

Why this replaced a mintable dollar

The dollar on this chain used to be a test token with an unguarded mint(). Anyone could create any amount, so its supply was arbitrary, the price in the KHRt pool meant nothing, and it was simultaneously accepted as Aave collateral — anyone could have minted a pile and borrowed out the other side. That has been removed.

Every unit here was originated. Total supply is not a decision anybody makes; it is the sum of the vault's books, and the tests assert those two numbers stay equal.

It is inside the compliance perimeter, and a bridged dollar cannot be. docs/architecture.md §7.1 accepts that a bridged token arrives with no identity hook and no freeze, because it is somebody else's contract minted by somebody else's bridge. A currency CSB issues itself checks the identity registry on every transfer — including issuance. That is the strongest argument for minting foreign currency here rather than importing it, and it is worth more than the economics.

What bounds the damage is the ceiling, not the ratio. A collateral ratio protects against a rate that moves; nothing about a ratio protects against one that is wrong. Reproduce all of it: npx hardhat test test/currency-vault.test.js — see docs/currency.md.
